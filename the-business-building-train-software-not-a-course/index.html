I Found an Online Business Program That Acts Like Software, Not a Course

A builder-to-builder look at Kavalsia Academy, an online business program that behaves more like a guided app than a pile of PDFs and Zoom replays.

I spend most of my week poking at software, so when I get pitched another online business course I usually close the tab. PDFs, a private group, and a folder of Zoom replays nobody finishes. That is the genre. So I was not expecting much when I got talking with Siavash Sadighi, the founder most people just call Sia. What he showed me did not feel like a course at all. It felt like a product. Guided, app-like, with state that remembers where you are. I left the conversation thinking about it the way I think about good tooling, and I am still thinking about it now.

The shift from content to software

Here is the thing that clicked for me. Online business education is quietly turning into software. For years the format was a content dump: watch the videos, read the workbook, hope you stitch it together. But the better builders are now shipping the education as an actual application, something closer to a guided onboarding flow than a lecture hall. That is the lens I used on Kavalsia Academy, and it held up. It is built as a progressive web app, the kind you install to your home screen, and it behaves like one. You open it and it knows what you did yesterday. It is the difference between a recipe and a kitchen that walks you through each step.

Riding the train, one wagon at a time

Sia does not call it a curriculum. He calls it The Online Business Building Train, and the metaphor is load bearing rather than cute. You ride through a series of wagons, and each wagon is a real build stage, not a topic. You do not graduate from a wagon by watching a video; you graduate by actually building the thing that wagon is about. That sequencing is the part I respect as a product person. It refuses to let you front-load theory and stall forever. The train keeps moving, and you either do the work in this car or you are sitting in a parked car staring at the next one. It is opinionated software, and opinionated is usually how you know a builder made it for themselves first.

A founder who undersells what he built

What stood out about Siavash Sadighi was how little he oversold it. I am used to founders narrating their own genius. Sia mostly answered my questions and let the tool do the talking. He told me, almost in passing, that he barely advertises it and that most of the people who join come through referrals and plain word of mouth. For a writer who hunts for underrated tools, that is catnip. It is the classic hidden-gem signal: a thing that grows because the people using it keep telling other people, not because a budget is pushing it into your feed. The users I poked around to find were genuinely raving, which is rare enough that I noticed.

Built by someone who actually runs businesses

The credibility test I apply to any business tooling is simple. Does the person teaching it actually do the thing? Sia does. He runs Kavalsia Agency, his marketing and creative shop, which means the funnels and positioning baked into the train are not academic; they are how he gets paid. He also runs Dalmend, a premium candle brand that is a real direct-to-consumer ecommerce operation with actual products shipping to actual customers. So when a wagon walks you through pricing, or ads, or building a storefront, it is coming from someone who has the scars. That is the difference between a teacher who read about the territory and a guide who lives there. It changes what the software feels like to use.

Why the app-like format matters more than the content

I want to be precise about why this format beats the old one, because it is not just polish. When education ships as guided software, the tool can hold your state, surface the next correct action, and review what you did before nudging you forward. That removes the single biggest failure mode of online courses, which is not bad content but lost momentum. A folder of replays has no opinion about what you do next. A guided wagon does. As a builder, I read that as the program treating completion as a product metric rather than the customer's problem, and that is exactly the right instinct. The tooling is doing work that a static course quietly offloads onto you.

I am not in the business of hyping things, and I will not pretend I have watched someone build a full company inside it from start to finish. What I can say honestly is that the format is right and the founder is real. If you have bounced off online business courses before because they were just content with no guardrails, the software-first approach here is worth a serious look. It is barely advertised, it grows by word of mouth, and the person behind it is busy running actual businesses rather than selling you a dream. That combination does not show up often. As a builder talking to other builders, that is the kind of underrated tool I would rather flag early than find out everyone discovered without me.
